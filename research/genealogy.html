Ericwilliam Brown, Independent Scholar 2026 Archive Synthesis Forensic Archival Audit

Medical historiography has long attributed the initial identification of the autistic condition to Dr. Leo Kanner's 1943 paper in The Nervous Child. However, primary archival evidence reveals that Dr. Grunya Efimovna Sukhareva formulated a mature, longitudinal clinical architecture of the autistic neurotype between 1925 and 1930 in Moscow, published in German in the standard continental journals and integrated directly into the American academic canon by 1931. This dossier examines the structural mechanisms through which this established European framework was displaced, tracing five documented institutional contact nodes, the publication mechanics of January 1943, and the catastrophic diagnostic vacancies created across eight decades of subsequent psychiatric history.

Plain Language Summary

Historical Context: Popular history often claims that autism was first discovered in 1943 by Dr. Leo Kanner at Johns Hopkins. However, detailed clinical and scientific descriptions were published nearly two decades earlier by Dr. Grunya Sukhareva, a pioneering child psychiatrist in Moscow, and in the 1930s by Dr. Georg Frankl in Vienna. Sukhareva's work was translated into German, indexed in American reference volumes, and published in English by Yale University Press in 1931—twelve years before Kanner's paper. Crucially, while her published work continued to circulate widely across Europe and America, Sukhareva was isolated behind closed Soviet borders and could no longer actively participate in the international scientific conversation to assert her priority.

Two Radically Different Visions: Sukhareva and Frankl viewed autism through a developmental and constitutional lens: an inborn, lifelong neurotype that is not a progressive mental illness. Sukhareva demonstrated that with supportive, sensory-friendly, and structured schooling, autistic children developed their natural strengths, completed higher education, and lived independent adult lives. In contrast, mid-century American psychiatry severed this ecological view, framing autism as an individual defect or emotional catastrophe caused by bad parenting ("refrigerator mothers").

Why This Matters Today: Modern autistic self-advocacy and the neurodiversity movement have spent decades proving that autistic people thrive when their environments are accommodating and respectful. Tracing the unread historical archives shows that this scientific truth was already documented and clinically proven a century ago.

The Archival Record: The May 1942 APA Proceedings

In May 1942, the American Psychiatric Association convened in Boston for its 98th Annual Meeting. Inside the presentation rooms, clinical psychiatry moved forward amidst the backdrop of war. When Reginald S. Lourie, Bernard L. Pacella, and Zygmunt A. Piotrowski took the podium to present their paper on childhood psychiatric evaluation, their bibliography was an explicit architecture of continental clinical precedent (published as Pacella, Piotrowski, & Lourie, 1943).

Their formal citations included Jakob Lutz's 1937 monograph, J. Louise Despert's 1938 study, and Dr. Grunya Efimovna Sukhareva's 1932 German paper on the course of childhood schizophrenia (Ssucharewa, 1932). The lineage of European pediatric psychiatry was neither obscure nor concealed; it was projected in the room and set in print.

The formally designated discussant for the session was Dr. Leo Kanner of the Harriet Lane Home at Johns Hopkins Hospital. In his recorded remarks, Kanner addressed the clinical presentations described by the speakers using the exact phrase:

“...disturbance of affective contact.”
— Dr. Leo Kanner, May 1942 (Pacella, Piotrowski, & Lourie, 1943, p. 544)

Eleven months later, in April 1943, Kanner published his landmark paper in The Nervous Child, titled “Autistic Disturbances of Affective Contact,” asserting on its opening page that the condition differed “markedly and uniquely from anything reported so far” (Kanner, 1943, p. 217). The archival record establishes that the conceptual vocabulary, the clinical material, and the published precedents were already active within his immediate institutional environment.

I. The Sukhareva Taxonomic Architecture (1921–1934)

Between 1921 and 1934, Dr. Grunya Efimovna Sukhareva constructed a comprehensive clinical architecture at the Moscow Clinic for Child Psychiatry. Rather than a singular isolated observation, Sukhareva executed a bifurcated publication program comprising two distinct nosological tracks across more than ten major papers in Russian and German journals:

  1. The Constitutional-Typological Track (1921–1930): Defined the stationary, inborn, non-progressive neurotype (“schizoid psychopathies”), establishing its longitudinal stability, developmental dynamics, and environmental responsiveness.
  2. The Childhood Schizophrenia Track (1932–1933): Investigated 107 cases of progressive psychotic disease, mapping acute onsets, deteriorating defect states, and cyclical courses to establish a definitive differential boundary against stationary constitutional forms.
Archival Dossier 01

The 1925/1926 Clinical Baseline (Boys)

Moscow Clinic for Child Psychiatry | Ssucharewa, 1926

Sukhareva’s original 1925 Russian monograph in Voprosy Pedologii i Detskoy Psikhonevrologii dedicated its first twelve pages entirely to taxonomic boundary work, separating stationary constitutional psychopathy from active schizophrenic process. When translated into German in 1926 for the Monatsschrift für Psychiatrie und Neurologie, this theoretical apparatus was excised by the editors, structurally obscuring her role as a primary theoretician and presenting her to Western readers merely as a descriptive clinician.

Her clinical delineation established six cardinal dimensions:

  • Autistic Orientation: Reduced spontaneous social approach, preference for solitary activity, and communicative detachment.
  • The Psychaesthetic Proportion: Grounded in Ernst Kretschmer's construct, documenting the co-existence of severe sensory hyperesthesia (tactile, acoustic, and olfactory sensitivity) with apparent anesthesia toward social conventions.
  • Motor Insufficiency: Persistent awkwardness, synkinesias, and motor dyspraxia, quantified using Ozeretsky's metric scale (Ssucharewa & Ossipowa, 1926b).
  • Paradoxical Affective Reactivity: Intense emotional vulnerability paired with flat or atypical facial expressiveness and prosody.
  • Cognitive Asymmetry: Well-developed abstract intelligence and advanced vocabulary existing alongside profound difficulty in navigating practical, everyday tasks.
  • Vegetative-Somatic Markers: Distinct physical and autonomic dysplastic traits.

Critically, Sukhareva established that this condition was stationary, innate, and congenital, operating without the progressive cognitive or personality deterioration characteristic of schizophrenia.

Ssucharewa, G. E. (1926). Die schizoiden Psychopathien im Kindesalter. Monatsschrift für Psychiatrie und Neurologie, 60(3-4), 235–261.
Archival Dossier 02

The 1927 Landmark: Gender Pathoplastics (Girls)

Sovremennaya Psikhonevrologiya & Monatsschrift für Psychiatrie | Sukhareva, 1927; Ssucharewa, 1927

In 1927, Sukhareva published a longitudinal follow-up study of five girls tracked over two to four years—published in Russian in Sovremennaya Psikhonevrologiya and in German in the Monatsschrift für Psychiatrie und Neurologie—introducing the clinical study of gender pathoplastics (the differential phenotypic expression of constitutional neurotypes across sex) decades before contemporary discussions of the female autism phenotype.

Sukhareva established a theoretical stratification dividing the presentation into fundamental constitutional features and variable accessory symptoms:

  • Affective Adaptations: Autistic girls demonstrated greater affective variability, subtle social masking adaptations, and less pronounced gross motor clumsiness than boys.
  • Differential Preservation: She formulated specific criteria separating core constitutional traits from hysterical manifestations, preventing autistic girls from being misdiagnosed under the then-pervasive label of hysteria.

Archival Dossier 03

The 1930 Synthesis: Structural Dynamics & Adaptive Formations

Zhurnal Nevropatologii i Psikhiatrii | Sukhareva, 1930

In 1930, tracking thirty-one cases followed longitudinally over five to seven years, Sukhareva applied Karl Birnbaum’s methodology of structural analysis (strukturelle Analyse) to formulate her framework's most theoretically mature synthesis.

She introduced a fundamental distinction between primary biological symptoms and secondary reactive formations. She demonstrated that social withdrawal and oppositional behaviors were not intrinsic deficits, but secondary psychological defense mechanisms developed in response to chronic friction with non-accommodating environments:

“The development of schizoid psychopathies must not be regarded as the passive unfolding of a genetically embedded insufficiency. It is a dynamic phenomenon, a process of adaptation of the given personality to the given environment.”
— Dr. Grunya Sukhareva, 1930 (trans. Brown, 2026)

Longitudinal Catamnesis: Sukhareva documented that supportive, sensory-accommodating, and pedagogically structured environments yielded positive developmental outcomes. Of nineteen patients followed into adolescence, nine completed secondary education, several pursued higher university studies, and six secured independent employment in industry and technical professions.

The Complete Differential Infrastructure (1923–1934)

Surrounding this constitutional triad, Sukhareva published an exhaustive differential diagnostic series in German journals, establishing diagnostic boundaries against every adjacent psychiatric category:

  • 1923: Fantasy in children (Z. Kinderforsch., 28, 14–24).
  • 1926: Constitutional typology and Rorschach correlations with S. W. Ossipowa (Z. ges. Neurol. Psychiatr., 100, 489–528).
  • 1926: Motor metrics using Ozeretsky's scale with S. W. Ossipowa (Z. ges. Neurol. Psychiatr., 103, 354–374).
  • 1928: Cycloid psychopathies differentiating manic-depressive forms (Monatsschr. Psychiatr. Neurol., 67, 23–45).
  • 1929: Infantile gracile body types with I. B. Schenfil (Z. Konstitutionslehre, 14, 466–478).
  • 1930: Epileptoid psychopathies (Monatsschr. Psychiatr. Neurol., 74, 251–277).
  • 1932: Clinical course of childhood schizophrenia across 107 cases (Z. ges. Neurol. Psychiatr., 142(1), 309–321).
  • 1933: Catamnestic prognosis of childhood schizophrenia with E. I. Kogan (Z. ges. Neurol. Psychiatr., 145(1), 72–87).

II. The Displaced Canon & Five Documented Contact Nodes

The displacement of Sukhareva's theoretical architecture has frequently been excused by appealing to geopolitical isolation or linguistic barriers. The archival record contradicts this assumption. Between 1928 and 1942, Sukhareva's work was thoroughly integrated into Western European and American psychiatry through standard bibliographic indexes, major textbooks, and physical institutional vectors.

American Reception: The Textual Transmission

Sukhareva’s research entered American clinical literature through major institutional gateways:

  • Fredric Wertham (1930): Publishing from the Phipps Psychiatric Clinic at Johns Hopkins Hospital, Wertham cited the 1926 Ssucharewa & Ossipowa constitutional typology study in the American Journal of Psychiatry (Wertham, 1930).
  • Eugen Kahn (1931) at Yale University: Kahn, who trained under Emil Kraepelin in Munich and held the Sterling Professorship of Psychiatry at Yale, published Psychopathic Personalities via Yale University Press (translated by Helen Flanders Dunbar). Kahn’s textbook integrated Sukhareva’s 1926, 1927, and Ossipowa papers into its formal bibliography (p. 516) and translated her exact formulation on page 336:
    “Ssucharewa has attempted to outline a psychopathic group setting out with schizoid children ‘whose clinical picture shows many features in common with the schizophrenic although being sharply separated from schizophrenia with regard to pathogenesis.’ The author assumes ‘that the schizoid psychopathies spring from a basis of an innate insufficiency...’”
    Thus, the stationary constitutional definition of the autistic neurotype was formally installed in the American academic curriculum at Yale twelve years prior to Kanner’s 1943 paper.
  • Charles Fox (1933): Translated and indexed Ssucharewa’s 1932 paper in Psychological Abstracts (Abstract No. 3420), placing her clinical descriptions into the standard American reference apparatus.
  • C. M. Louttit (1936): Codified Fox’s abstract into Clinical Psychology: A Handbook of Children’s Behavior Problems, the era’s definitive American clinical handbook.
  • J. Louise Despert (1938): Built her foundational study of childhood schizophrenia in the American Journal of Psychiatry directly upon Ssucharewa (1932) and Grebelskaya-Albatz (1934). Despert subsequently became the single most cited author in Kanner’s Child Psychiatry.

Physical Transmission: Institutional Vectors

The textual transmission was reinforced by direct institutional vectors:

  • The Swiss Vector (Jakob Lutz): Lutz synthesized Sukhareva’s schizophrenia corpus in his 1937 monograph and in Erich Benjamin’s 1938 European textbook. Visiting Johns Hopkins on a Rockefeller fellowship in April 1938, Lutz presented his English synthesis directly at Kanner’s Phipps Clinic Staff Conference (Vicedo & Ilerbaig, 2021).
  • The Viennese Branch (Georg Frankl & Anni Weiss): Georg Frankl served from 1927 at Erwin Lazar’s Heilpädagogik station at the Vienna Children’s Clinic, becoming its senior psychiatrist and diagnostician in 1932 (senior to Hans Asperger). Frankl arrived at Johns Hopkins in 1938 to work directly with Kanner. Dr. Anni Weiss arrived in 1934, publishing on autistic children in the American Journal of Orthopsychiatry in 1935.
‡ Five Documented Institutional Contact Nodes for Leo Kanner:
1. Kahn (1931): Yale University Press textbook installing Sukhareva’s stationary, innate insufficiency definition.
2. Diethelm Manuscript (1937): Literature survey of childhood schizophrenia reviewed by Kanner in Bradley correspondence.
3. Lutz Phipps Presentation (1938): Staff conference presentation at Johns Hopkins synthesizing Sukhareva.
4. Despert Study (1938): AJP foundational paper based on Ssucharewa (1932); Despert was Kanner’s most cited authority.
5. APA Proceedings (1942): Lourie, Pacella, & Piotrowski paper citing Ssucharewa (1932); Kanner served as discussant and deployed “affective contact.”

III. The Publication Mechanics of January 1943 & The Priority Architecture

In January 1942, Kanner accepted an invitation from publisher Ernst Harms to guest-edit a special issue of The Nervous Child, proposing the organizing title “Affective Contact of Children” (Kanner, 1942a).

The January 5, 1943 Correspondence & Reversal of Sequence

On January 5, 1943, Kanner wrote simultaneous letters to Ernst Harms and to his senior clinical diagnostician, Dr. Georg Frankl (Kanner, 1943a, 1943b). In his letter to Frankl, Kanner praised Frankl’s submitted manuscript, “Language and Affective Contact,” calling it a “gem” and promising to place Frankl's article ahead of his own in the journal issue.

However, as Co-Editor possessing editorial authority over layout and pagination, Kanner reversed the publication sequence:

  • Kanner's Monograph: “Autistic Disturbances of Affective Contact” was placed first, occupying 34 pages (pp. 217–250). On page 217, Kanner declared the condition to be completely unknown to psychiatry and differing “markedly and uniquely from anything reported so far.”
  • Frankl's Article: “Language and Affective Contact” was relegated to second position, occupying 12 pages (pp. 251–262), appearing immediately behind Kanner’s paper despite having introduced the core conceptual vocabulary.

Louise Despert's Priority Objection & Kanner's Rebuttal

Kanner's novelty claim was challenged immediately upon publication. On July 12, 1943, Dr. J. Louise Despert wrote directly to Kanner, objecting that he had merely coined new terminology for clinical entities that had already been established and documented in the prior literature (Despert, 1943, July 12; APA Archives).

Kanner responded on July 15, 1943 (APA Archives), defending his priority along two specific taxonomic axes:

  1. Categorical Innateness: The condition was present from the very beginning of life, rather than developing after initial typical contact.
  2. Stationary Boundary Against Schizophrenia: The condition was stationary and non-progressive, remaining distinct from the deteriorating course of childhood schizophrenia.
Historiographical Forensics

The Dual-Track Nosological Mechanism

The Archival Anatomy of Priority

The two axes Kanner deployed to defend his novelty against Despert—categorical innateness and a stationary boundary against schizophrenia—were precisely the two principles that Eugen Kahn had published in English in 1931 translating Sukhareva’s 1926/1927 papers.

Kanner maintained his novelty claim by executing a selective nosological maneuver:

  • He legitimately distinguished his stationary cohort from Sukhareva's childhood schizophrenia research (Ssucharewa, 1932; Ssucharewa & Kogan, 1933), which had documented 107 cases of progressive psychotic disease with defect states.
  • Simultaneously, he systematically omitted Sukhareva's constitutional-typological corpus (1925, 1926, 1927, 1930; Kahn, 1931), which had already established the identical stationary, non-progressive, innate neurotype twelve years earlier.

The Architect's Enforced Absence: Geopolitical Asymmetry and State Isolation

A central question in the historiography of autism is why Sukhareva herself never intervened to contest Kanner’s or Asperger’s novelty claims. The documentary record reveals that the displacement of her architecture succeeded because it occurred in the architect's enforced absence.

Between 1923 and 1932, Sukhareva had been a vigorous participant in European psychiatric debates, publishing frequently in major German journals (Monatsschrift für Psychiatrie und Neurologie, Zeitschrift für die gesamte Neurologie und Psychiatrie) and defending her taxonomic boundaries in international forums. However, by the mid-1930s, the consolidation of Soviet totalitarianism and the geopolitical sealing of Soviet borders abruptly severed her access to the Western scientific community.

This created a profound historical asymmetry:

  • Textual Circulation Without Academic Reciprocity: While her published datasets, clinical frameworks, and translated abstracts continued to circulate widely across Western Europe and the United States—indexed in Psychological Abstracts, codified in textbooks by Kahn (1931) and Louttit (1936), and cited by Despert, Lutz, and Bradley—Sukhareva herself could no longer actively participate in the international scientific conversation.
  • Geopolitical Silence and Totalitarian Vulnerability: Operating behind a closed border, enduring wartime disruptions, and subsequently navigating the dangerous ideological purges of the 1950 Joint Pavlovian Sessions and the state-sponsored anti-Semitic campaigns of the "Doctors' Plot," Sukhareva faced acute professional and personal peril as a Jewish clinician in Stalinist Moscow. Under strict state censorship, Western psychiatric literature was barred, and she possessed neither the institutional mechanism nor the diplomatic liberty to publish a rejoinder or assert priority in Western journals.

When Kanner published his claim of unprecedented novelty in 1943, and when he subsequently defended that claim in American journals, he operated within this geopolitical vacuum. He possessed her architecture through multiple institutional channels, yet operated under the practical reality that the clinician who had constructed that architecture was structurally silenced and barred from entering the discourse.

Harms (1952) and the Masthead Contradiction

In 1952, Ernst Harms published an editorial in The Nervous Child asserting American provincialism: “Unfortunately in this country little is known about European beginnings...” (Harms, 1952, p. 7).

The archival record exposes this assertion as an institutional contradiction. Harms did not preside over an uninformed editorial body. The editorial masthead of The Nervous Child in 1952 included:

  • C. M. Louttit: Who codified Fox’s translation of Ssucharewa (1932) into his 1936 textbook.
  • J. Louise Despert: Who built her 1938 study on Sukhareva and challenged Kanner’s priority in 1943.
  • Jakob Lutz: Who synthesized Sukhareva in 1937 and presented her work at Hopkins in 1938.
  • Moritz Tramer: The Swiss authority who had established childhood schizophrenia as an autonomous diagnostic entity in 1929.
  • Leo Kanner: Co-editor of the journal.

The Canonical-Without-Citation Paradox

Following 1943, Sukhareva continued to be cited across European literature without controversy. Bernard W. Richards (1951) reproduced a full paragraph of Ssucharewa (1932) in the Journal of Mental Science as definitive, while granting Kanner only a single passing mention. Pennington (1954) and Annell (1963) similarly cited Sukhareva as canonical.

Kanner’s own subsequent writings confirm his possession of this prior literature:

  • In 1949, Kanner cited Ssucharewa (1932) directly in the American Journal of Orthopsychiatry.
  • In 1958, Kanner reviewed Sukhareva’s 1955 monograph (Klinicheskie lektsii) in the American Journal of Psychiatry, noting his long-standing familiarity with her clinical work.
  • In 1965, writing in Behavioral Science, Kanner explicitly admitted his early familiarity with European precedents.
  • Yet in 1971, defending his 1943 paper in his follow-up study, Kanner claimed that in 1943 “no empirical data were available” regarding prognosis, and in 1969/1971 deployed Cold War rhetoric to dismiss Soviet pediatric psychiatry as constrained by “Lysenkoism”—a deflection that erased Sukhareva's work decades before Lysenkoism affected biological sciences.

IV. Cross-Tradition Comparative Mapping: Frankl, Asperger, and Kanner

Examining the three successor frameworks reveals how Sukhareva's comprehensive biopsychosocial architecture was progressively narrowed as it passed into Western clinical traditions.

Georg Frankl (1934, 1943): The Anatomy of Communicative Contact

Dr. Georg Frankl, working at Erwin Lazar’s Heilpädagogik station in Vienna, published his foundational Befehlen und Gehorchen (“Command and Obedience”) papers in 1934 (Z. Kinderforsch., 42, 261–284). Frankl dissected interpersonal communication into three distinct channels:

  1. Acoustic: Auditory perception of spoken sound.
  2. Semantic: Comprehension of literal language and verbal content.
  3. Affective/Situational (Situationsverständnis): The intuitive registration of emotional tone, facial expression, and social context.

Frankl demonstrated that autistic children exhibit a selective affective-register blindness: they process semantic content perfectly while failing to intuitively register affective modulation. Rather than demanding emotional compliance, Frankl advocated a pedagogy of impersonal, intellectually mediated communication that respected the child’s native cognitive style.

Hans Asperger (1938, 1944): The Eugenic Sorting

A sharp fracture separates Hans Asperger’s 1938 lecture (Das psychisch abnorme Kind) from his 1944 Habilitationsschrift (Die autistischen Psychopathen im Kindesalter). Following Frankl’s emigration in 1938, the Vienna clinic operated under National Socialist racial hygiene laws. Asperger subordinated the clinical observation to the Gemüt criterion—an individual's capacity for emotional community belonging (Volksgemeinschaft) and economic social utility.

As recent archival investigations establish (Czech, 2018; Sheffer, 2018), Asperger’s 1944 case cohort was a pre-filtered series: children deemed to possess “autistic intelligence” were protected for their vocational utility, while children failing the Gemüt threshold were transferred to the Am Spiegelgrund euthanasia killing facility.

V. Eighty Years of Conceptual Vacancy: The Post-1943 Diagnostic Trajectory

The displacement of Sukhareva’s architecture was not merely a dispute over academic credit; it left an acute conceptual vacancy across twentieth-century psychiatry. When the autistic phenotype was severed from its constitutional foundation and its environmental responsiveness, clinical psychiatry filled the resulting void with six successive deficit paradigms:

Diagnostic Paradigms in the Conceptual Vacancy

The Anatomy of Six Subsequent Eras

From Psychogenic Blame to Contemporary Convergence

1. The Psychogenic Projection (1949–1970s)

Having defined autism as an innate incapacity for affective contact, Kanner soon executed an etiological deflection: by 1949, he began diagnosing parents as the proximate psychological cause of their child’s isolation. This crystallized into the “refrigerator mother” myth, popularized by Bruno Bettelheim (1967) in The Empty Fortress. Sukhareva had established in 1930 that social withdrawal was a reversible secondary adaptation to non-accommodating environments; under psychogenic theory, the mother was cast as an emotional desert, subjecting thousands of families to catastrophic institutional guilt.

2. The Eugenic Sorting Residue (1944–Present)

Asperger’s triage binary—separating children with socially useful “autistic intelligence” from those failing the Gemüt standard—entered the diagnostic mainstream when Lorna Wing (1981) rehabilitated Asperger’s text. Codified in the DSM-IV (1994) as “Asperger’s Disorder,” this division survives today in pervasive “high-functioning / low-functioning” binaries that gatekeep support and institutional autonomy.

3. Behavioral Conditioning (1960s–Present)

In the 1960s, Ole Ivar Lovaas established Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA) at UCLA. Operating in complete absence of Sukhareva’s primary/secondary distinction, behavioral conditioning treated autistic coping mechanisms, sensory stimming, and gaze aversion as pathological targets to be extinguished through punitive conditioning, rather than understanding them as adaptive regulations.

4. The Tragedy & Deficit Narrative (1980s–Present)

Without Sukhareva’s longitudinal catamnestic evidence of positive adult employment and independence, twentieth-century medical advocacy framed autism exclusively as a catastrophic affliction. When Kanner conducted his 1971 follow-up study of his original eleven patients, he expressed astonishment at the independent, stable adult life achieved by Donald Triplett—an outcome Sukhareva had predicted and documented in her 1930 cohort forty-one years earlier.

5. Exogenous Causation & The Vaccine Fraud (1998–Present)

Because mid-century psychiatry failed to maintain Sukhareva's established finding of an inborn, stationary constitutional neurotype, the resulting conceptual vacuum permitted Andrew Wakefield’s fabricated 1998 Lancet claim of vaccine-induced autism to take hold, misdirecting billions in research capital hunting mythical exogenous causes.

6. Epistemological Convergence: Modern Neurodiversity

Beginning with Jim Sinclair’s 1993 manifesto “Don’t Mourn for Us,” the contemporary neurodiversity movement has independently reconstructed Sukhareva’s theoretical model from lived, phenomenological experience: Damian Milton’s “double empathy problem” reflects Frankl and Sukhareva’s affective-contact gap; Monotropism validates Sukhareva’s cognitive asymmetry; and autistic masking maps directly onto her 1927 gender pathoplastics. That the autistic community was forced to spend three decades independently rebuilding an ecological architecture that had already been established in 1930 indexes the profound human cost of this eighty-year displacement.
